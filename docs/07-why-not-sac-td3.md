# 7. Why PPO, and not SAC or TD3

This repository ships a comparison — `logs/algorithm_comparison.md` — concluding
that PPO is the right algorithm for this task. The conclusion happens to be
correct. The experiment does not support it.

This chapter does two things: explains the real difference between on-policy and
off-policy methods, and then re-reads that experiment honestly. The second part
is the more useful one, because learning to distrust your own benchmark is a
more transferable skill than learning which algorithm won.

## 7.1 On-policy versus off-policy

**On-policy** methods (REINFORCE, A2C, TRPO, PPO) learn from data generated by
the current policy. Chapter 5 showed why: the surrogate objective is only valid
near $\theta_\text{old}$. Data is used for a few epochs and discarded.

**Off-policy** methods (DQN, DDPG, TD3, SAC) learn from a replay buffer
containing data from many past policies. They can do this because they learn
$Q(s,a)$, and the Bellman optimality equation (2.8),

$$
Q^*(s,a) = \mathbb{E}_{s'}\!\left[ r + \gamma \max_{a'} Q^*(s',a') \right]
$$

does not reference the policy that generated the transition at all. Any
$(s,a,r,s')$ tuple is a valid training example for $Q^*$, no matter who
produced it.

The consequences:

| | On-policy (PPO) | Off-policy (SAC, TD3) |
|---|---|---|
| Sample reuse | a few epochs, then discarded | indefinite, from a replay buffer |
| Sample efficiency | low | high |
| Wall-clock efficiency with a fast simulator | **high** | often lower |
| Parallelism | scales almost linearly in envs | limited by gradient-step throughput |
| Stability | good; the trust region is explicit | fragile; bootstrapping off a learned $\max_a Q$ |
| Sensitivity to reward scale | low (advantages are standardised) | high ($Q$ magnitudes enter directly) |

## 7.2 The argument that actually decides it

The usual framing is "off-policy is more sample-efficient", and it is true. It
is also the wrong axis for this problem.

Sample efficiency matters when samples are expensive — a real robot, a slow
simulator, a human in the loop. Here, a sample costs ten MuJoCo physics steps.
On the machine this repository was developed on, one environment produces about
810 control steps per second, and sixteen in parallel produce about 620
samples/s end to end including the gradient updates. On a GPU with MJX
(chapter 17) that number goes to hundreds of thousands.

When samples are nearly free, the binding constraint is **gradient steps per
unit of wall-clock time**, and the picture inverts:

- PPO does one batched forward pass across all 16 environments, then 20
  gradient steps on 8192 transitions. Adding environments costs almost nothing
  on the learning side.
- SAC does one gradient step per environment step by default, and each involves
  two Q-networks, two target networks, a policy, and an entropy-temperature
  update. Scaling to 4096 parallel environments means either 4096 gradient
  steps per environment step (absurd) or a replay ratio so low that the
  sample-efficiency advantage evaporates.

There is a second argument, less often stated and at least as important for
this task. Off-policy methods bootstrap through a learned $\max_{a'} Q(s', a')$.
In a contact-rich system, $Q$ has sharp structure — a foot lands or it does not
— and function approximators overestimate maxima of noisy functions.
TD3's two headline features (clipped double-Q, delayed policy updates) exist
entirely to fight this overestimation, and they only partly succeed. PPO never
takes a maximum over actions, so the failure mode does not exist.

## 7.3 Where off-policy genuinely wins

To be fair to the methods being dismissed:

- **Real-robot learning.** When each sample costs seconds of wall clock and
  hardware wear, replay is decisive.
- **Sparse rewards with long horizons.** Replay lets rare successes be
  revisited many times; PPO sees a rare success once and throws it away.
- **Small-scale problems.** With one environment and a slow simulator, SAC
  will usually beat PPO on the same wall-clock budget.

And the honest note: SAC in particular is a very strong algorithm on
locomotion benchmarks when tuned properly. The literature preference for PPO in
massively-parallel legged locomotion (Isaac Gym, legged_gym, MJX) is a preference
about *the parallel regime*, not a claim that PPO is a better algorithm.

## 7.4 Re-reading this repository's comparison

The shipped experiment:

> 1,000,000 timesteps each, single environment (`DummyVecEnv`), same
> environment, reward, and PD controller, `cmd_vel = [0.5, 0, 0]`, no curriculum.

| Metric | PPO | SAC | TD3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mean reward | 182.78 | 232.45 | **259.39** |
| Mean episode length | 264.2 | 243.7 | **440.2** |
| Survival rate | 0.0% | 0.0% | **10.0%** |
| Mean forward speed | **0.140** | 0.027 | 0.012 |

and the conclusion: *"PPO is the best algorithm for this task."*

There are four problems, in ascending order of severity.

### 7.4.1 TD3 was run without exploration noise

`scripts/compare_algorithms.py` constructs TD3 with no `action_noise` argument.
Stable-Baselines3 defaults `action_noise=None`, and TD3's policy is
**deterministic**. Unlike SAC, which explores through its entropy-regularised
stochastic policy, TD3 has no other source of exploration.

So TD3 was run with essentially no exploration at all, on a continuous
12-dimensional control problem. Its result is not a measurement of TD3; it is a
measurement of what a deterministic policy does when it never tries anything
new. That it converged to standing still is the expected outcome and tells us
nothing about the algorithm.

The standard fix is `action_noise=NormalActionNoise(mean=zeros(12), sigma=0.1*ones(12))`.

### 7.4.2 Equal timesteps is not equal compute, and favours neither fairly

The three were given 1M environment steps each. But:

- PPO with `n_steps=2048`, `n_epochs=10`, `batch_size=64` did
  $10 \times 2048/64 = 320$ gradient steps per rollout, so ~156,000 gradient
  steps over the run.
- SAC and TD3 with `train_freq=1, gradient_steps=1` did one gradient step per
  environment step: 1,000,000 gradient steps, each over two critics and a
  target network.

The off-policy runs used several times more compute for the same "budget".
Equal-timesteps comparisons are conventional, but they should be reported
alongside wall-clock, and here they were not.

### 7.4.3 Single environment is PPO's worst case

The comparison used `DummyVecEnv` with one environment, then concluded that PPO
is best — while noting in the analysis that PPO "benefits most from parallel
envs (8x in the main training run)". The experiment therefore measured the
configuration least favourable to the winner, and the reasoning for the
conclusion comes from a *different* run with different settings. Whatever this
experiment shows, it is not what the conclusion says it shows.

### 7.4.4 The environment was broken for all three

This is the one that voids the result entirely.

All three algorithms were trained on the v1 environment, in which
`default_joint_pos` was `qpos0` — all twelve joints at zero — rather than the
Go2's standing pose (chapter 14, bug B1). With `action_scale = 0.5`, no policy
of any kind could command the calf angle of $-1.8$ rad the robot needs to
stand, let alone walk.

The experiment did not compare three algorithms on a locomotion task. It
compared three algorithms on a task where locomotion was **kinematically
unreachable**, and measured which one degraded most gracefully. The mean forward
speeds — 0.14, 0.027, 0.012 m/s — are all essentially zero. Ranking them is
ranking noise.

### The one thing the numbers do show

Look again at the reward column. TD3 scored the **highest** mean reward (259)
while achieving the **lowest** forward speed (0.012 m/s). SAC scored higher than
PPO while moving five times slower.

That is not an algorithm result. It is a **reward-function result**: under v1's
reward, standing still scored better than trying to walk. The alive bonus was
0.5/step and the velocity-tracking term maxed at 2.0/step but was multiplied
by an exponential that a stationary robot still collected $\exp(-0.25/0.25) = 0.37$
of, i.e. 0.74/step for free. Meanwhile every penalty term punished the motion
required to actually walk.

So the comparison table is real evidence — for chapter 10's argument about
reward design, not for chapter 7's argument about algorithms. This is worth
sitting with: the experiment contained a genuine, important finding, and it was
filed under the wrong conclusion.

## 7.5 How to run the comparison properly

If you want to redo it:

1. **Fix the environment first.** Run it on v2, where standing and walking are
   both reachable.
2. **Give TD3 exploration noise.** `NormalActionNoise(sigma=0.1)`.
3. **Match compute, not just samples.** Report wall-clock alongside timesteps,
   and set SAC/TD3's `gradient_steps` to something defensible.
4. **Run each configuration at the environment count it is designed for.** PPO
   at 16+ envs, SAC/TD3 at 1–4. Comparing them at a single shared env count
   guarantees you handicap one of them.
5. **Multiple seeds.** Three at minimum. Single-seed RL results are close to
   meaningless; between-seed variance on this task easily exceeds the
   between-algorithm differences reported above.
6. **Measure what you care about.** Under v2 the right metric is tracking error
   across the command envelope (`scripts/evaluate.py --grid`), not forward speed
   at one fixed command.

`scripts/compare_algorithms.py` still exists and still runs. Treat its output as
a starting point for that experiment, not as a finished result.

## 7.6 Summary

PPO is the right choice here, for reasons that are about the *regime* rather
than about algorithmic superiority: samples are cheap, parallelism is the
scaling axis, the reward is dense, and the contact dynamics make a learned
$\max_a Q$ a liability.

The shipped comparison reached that conclusion without supporting it. Keeping
that finding in the documentation, rather than quietly deleting the file, is the
point — an experiment that does not support its conclusion is a normal thing to
produce, and noticing it afterwards is the skill worth having.
